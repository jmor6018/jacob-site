imputed 1911 birth histories (100,000-woman sample)

Published

October 13, 2026

This is a sample of a set of full birth histories for 3.4 million married women in England and Wales in 1911. This means that the data records the age the woman was when she gave birth to all of her children, and can be used for a range of demographic analyses. The data here is the outcome of ~4,500 lines of raw data processing and imputation code, which works by using a hierarchy of many iterative conditional probabilities. These estimate when in a woman’s life she would have given birth to children that were once born to her but are now unobservable, either due to death or depature from the home.

Name: 100,000 sample of imputed birth histories from 1911 English and Welsh Census

Source: Schurer, K., Higgs, E., FINDMYPAST LIMITED (2024). Integrated Census Microdata (I-CeM), 1851-1911. [data collection]. UK Data Service. SN: 7481, DOI: https://doi.org/10.5255/UKDA-SN-7481-3.

Provenance of underlying census data: I-CeM is an enriched database of over 210 million individual standardised historical census records for Great Britain for the period 1851 to 1921, detailing characteristics for all individuals resident at the following censuses:

England and Wales: census of 1851, 1861, 1881, 1891, 1901, 1911, and 1921.

Scotland: census of 1851, 1861, 1871, 1881, 1891, 1901, and 1921.

Metadata and documentation for the integrated dataset of most of the censuses of Great Britain for the period 1851 to 1921: England and Wales for 1851-1861, 1881-1921 and Scotland for 1851-1901 is available at Integrated Census Microdata (I-CeM), 1851-1911. Metadata and documentation for the England and Wales 1921 data is available at Integrated Census Microdata (I-CeM), England and Wales, 1921.

I-CeM contains both original “strings” of transcribed data and coded data related to those strings. This coding process, having taken place over several years, enables filtered download of the data and standardises strings. In 1921 alone there were over 33,000 strings describing builders or bricklayers, only c.15% of which appeared more than once in the census. The coding process thus allows users to access similar data such as all those of certain occupations, genders, marital status, or birthplace with ease. Additionally, errors which occurred in the process of transcription have been “corrected” in the coded variables. Original strings have, however, not been altered and remain as transcribed. The method by which codes were constructed as well as problems which occurred with coding can be found here: (https://www.campop.geog.cam.ac.uk/research/projects/icem/construction) Lookup tables for enriched codes along with a full list of variables (original and coded) can be found here (https://www.campop.geog.cam.ac.uk/research/projects/icem/metadata).

What is this data?

The data here is the outcome of ¬4,500 lines of raw data processing and imputation, which works by using a hierarchy of many conditional probabilities to estimate when in a woman’s life she would have given birth to all the children I know she ever had, but who I can not observe.

The data here is at the mother level.

It contains barebones information on her birth year, how many of her ever-born children were still alive by 1911, and how many of them have died. Crucially, it contains a vector of ages separated by semicolons (;) which each denote the age the mother was when she gave birth to a given child.

How to use:

  • I will release this full script at the end of my DPhil so that the full underlying data can be replicated and used by others. However, for the use of the full dataset, other users or researchers will need to access:

    • The I-CeM Special License Edition by applying on the UK Data Service (UKDS).
    • 2025 ‘A First Full-Count Linking of English and Welsh Censuses, 1851-1921’, CWPESH, Guillaume Proffit, Alexis Litvine, Emma Diduch, (https://doi.org/10.17863/CAM.120530)

  • To use this sample data or the full edition without replication, you will first need to down a full raw edition of the 1911 census from I-CeM.

  • Then you will need to use the individual-level lookup between the ‘record id’ column (RecID) to match to the raw census inputs. This will allow you to perform meaningful multivariate analysis on the birth history outcomes using a matrix of census-derived predictors.

  • To learn about the socio-economic status of the women in the data, you are strongly urged to look up the RecIDs and census-derived variables of their co-resident husbands.

  • Geographic information is contained within the census (at the granular level of parish of enumeration under the version of I-CeM without streetname strings), but more useful registration subdistrict level information can be gained from: Reid, A., Jaadla, H., Schurer, K., & Garrett, E. (2025). Populations Past Data: Demographic and Socio-economic Data for Registration Sub-districts of England and Wales, 1851-1911, and Registration Districts of Scotland, 1851-1901. Apollo - University of Cambridge Repository. https://doi.org/10.17863/CAM.116164

  • Users will have to find their own chosen economic occupation categories to be proxied using the census-coded occupation variable. Examples include:

    • Clark, G., Cummins, N., & Curtis, M. (2024). Three new occupational status indices for England and Wales, 1800–1939. Historical Methods: A Journal of Quantitative and Interdisciplinary History, 57(1), 41–66. https://doi.org/10.1080/01615440.2024.2368458

Methodology:

A full methodology section can be request from the author (sjoh6018@ox.ac.uk).

The main imputing methodology uses a combination of approaches drawn from:

  • Dave Hacker (‘Reconstruction of birth histories using children ever born and children surviving data from the 1900 and 1910 U.S. censuses’, Historical Methods vol.53 28-52,2020 [https://doi.org/10.1080/01615440.2019.1664357]);
  • and Emma Diduch (‘Reconstructing birth histories using linked household data and the 1911 Census fertility survey’, Population Studies June 2026 1-15, [https://doi.org/10.1080/00324728.2026.2681475]);
  • and to a lesser extent Norman Luther and Lee-Jay Cho (‘Reconstruction of Birth Histories from Census and Household Survey Data’, Population Studies vol.42 no.3 451-472, 1988 [https://doi.org/10.1080/0032472031000143586])

The only important exception to this is that, in this sample, women who were linkable across multiple censuses based on immutable characteristics have been screened out. This is in accordance with data privacy requests from the authors of the longitudinal census links. This will attenuate coefficients produced by the data here and reduce overall accuracy.

The only major deviation from the above-listed methodologies comes from using a dataset of district-level mortality from 1851-1911, and a modified Brass-Logit method, to weight the conditional probabilities of birth-year assignment for dead children with local mortality conditions. It is outlined below.

Bespoke mortality calculations:

There is a newly available series of ‘consistent’ registration districts, which exploits the lesser propensity for the GRO to alter large RD administrative boundaries. The dataset links single or small groups of registration districts into areas which were sufficiently unchanged from 1851-1911 (Jaadla et al. 2023). A mortality series at the RD level – which numbered roughly 630 each census year – index matched to the series of consistent RDs (CONRD) would allow a series which could be used to adapt the series of life tables for imputation. For this reason, I reaggregated the RSD-level mortality series up to their higher registration districts using a simple population-weighted average (eq.5) for each census year, and reperformed the calculation for CONRDs which contained more than one RD. The result was a series of 557 CONRDs which possess IMR and ECMR data from 1851-1911. In the latest release of the CONRD data, a crucial series of adolescent, adult, and later-life mortality was also available (Reid et al. 2025). When stitched onto my reaggregated CONRD-level IMR and ECMR series, it provides consistent district level aggregate mortality from age 0 to 64.

〖Mortality〗_RD= ∑_(i=1)^n▒(RSD Population at Risk ×Mortality RSD)/(Total RD Population at Risk)

However, while IMR (deaths per 1000 in the first year of life) can be immediately used to adapt the first l𝑥 value, the other age brackets of mortality are a rate of death pooled all the ages. However, to turn these pooled averages into usable cumulative single-year mortality probabilities, demographic techniques which replicate a model life table must be used.

The method used here is an adapted Brass-logit method (Brass 1972). It approximates individual l𝑥 values through a relational logit model: two different mortality patterns, in this case the known standard cohort life tables for Victorian England and Wales from the Human Mortality Database (HMD 2026) and the target population of a registration district, can be linearly related using the log-odds of the cumulative death probabilities up to a given age (Murray et al. 2003, 4-6; Moultrie et al. 2013, 341-345). In both populations, the logit transformation of a cumulative death probability up to an age 𝑥 (l𝑥) in the known and target populations yields two straight lines which flatten the intervening mortality curves, and the comparison between their level shift in net mortality yields the α parameter, and their slope difference across the ages their β parameter. For the intervening ages in the target unknown population, the two parameters are plugged back into a reverse logit model drawn from the known population’s logit model (the model life table), and the curve is un-flattened to give an estimate of the cumulative death probability value up to that year (denoted Q𝑥). These values are then differenced using all the prior estimated cumulative death probabilities to yield single year l𝑥 (Wilmoth et al. 2012, tbl.4). The process is detailed below.

First, the probabilities of death to age 1 and 5 gained from the IMR and ECMR must be chained, and the raw rate-per-10,000 values for age-specific mortality from 5-14, 15-44, and 45-64 have to be turned into cumulative probabilities.

Q(5)=Q(1)+(1-Q(1))×4q1

Where Q represents the cumulative probability of dying before age 𝑥, and

Q(1)= IMR/1000 ,4q1= ECMR/1000

Where IMR and ECMR are the provided proportion not surviving the age window per 1000 entering it in the RSD-level data. Because the ASMRs for the three adolescent and adult age brackets are provided as population-level aggregate rates in the five years up to the census (death quantity as the numerator divided by person-years lived in that bracket), they need to be converted to cohort-based interval probabilities using average person-years survived in those age brackets at those census years from the national life table . This allows them to be differenced for cumulative probabilities. The weakness of this conversion assumes a similar age distribution nationally as in local areas, and a similar shape to within-bracket mortality patterns, but this cannot be avoided. It is given as:

〖(_n^)q〗_x= 〖n×(_n)m〗_x/(1+(n-〖(_n)a〗_x ) ×〖(_n^)m〗_x )

Where n is the width of the age interval in a bracket, and q is the probability of death in the bracket who survived to age x at the beginning of the bracket. Here m is the central death rate of people aged between x and x+n for each district drawn from the Populations Past decadal data, multiplied by 1000. Drawn from the life tables we need to know roughly the distribution of deaths across the age bracket to know the average person years lived a in the interval between age x and x+n but wo who died in it. If everyone dies on the first day a = 0, if everyone dies at the end a = n. This is estimated with:

〖(_n^)a〗_x= (〖(_n^)L〗_x-n × l_x+n)/(l_x-l_x+n)

Here, nLx provides the L person years lived (survivorship) between age x and x+n, and is simply the sum of all single-year survivorship probabilities Lx between age x and x+n drawn from the Human Mortality Database. Lowercase l is the whole-cohort cumulative survivorship to age x, meaning lx+n is the whole-cohort survivorship by the end of the interval. Equation 9 therefore takes all the person-years lived in the interval, and averages the contribution of the survivors to age x +n and the decedents within it.

Now, with the probabilities of death within each age interval calculated, the cumulative death chances accumulated to the end of each interval are given.

Q(x+n)=Q(x)+(1-Q(x))×〖(_n^)q〗_x

Where Q is the cumulative probability of dying by a given age. An example for the cumulative death probability by 45 is given below.

Q(45)=Q(15)+(1-Q(15))×〖(_30^)q〗_15

With the RD-specific cumulative death probabilities at the end of each anchor age interval, four iterations of the Brass-logit equation can be applied to get the logit transformed cumulative death probability Y(x).

Y(x)=ln⁡((Q(x))/(1-Q(x) ))

This is solved for Y(x) with the local (equations 5-11) and the national cumulative probabilities Qx and 1-Qx . This places both schedules on a common logit-transformed linear scale.

Y_Local (x)=α+ β×Y_National (x)

Where the logit transformed local RD values of Y are modelled as a linear function of the national life table schedule, giving α as the height of the line relative to the national standard, and β its slope across the local age patterns within the bracket between two known points (e.g. between Q(1) and Q(5)). Now the relational equation which adjusts the local to the national line allows us to solve for the two parameters and later for points on the line.

β_k= (Y_Local (x+n)- Y_Local (x))/(Y_National (x+n)- Y_National (x))

And:

α_k= Y_Local (x)- β_k×Y_National (x)

Where k represents one of the known brackets bounded by the ages 1-5, 5-15, 15-45, 45-65, and again x represents the age at the start of the bracket and n its width. When equations 14-15 are applied on the ages defining each bracket k for the national and local mortality schedules, the two schedules are related on the logit plane by the equation defined by parameters α and β for each age interval for each district.

Now, the Qx for ages within a bracket never observed in the districts can be solved for by logit transforming the Qx from a national schedule and applying the district’s given line equation.

Q_Local (x)=invlogit(αk+βk×Y_National (x))

This is performed for all individual ages x in each district, applying for each age the relevant parameters α and β from the age bracket k that x sits inside. With all the values of cumulative death probability Q, the inverse of cumulative survivorship l to each age x can be calculated, yielding the single year probabilities required for the imputation weighting.

l_x=1-Q(x)

The Brass-Logit method was applied iteratively four times given that each consistent registration district 1851-1911 has aggregate mortality data for the age brackets 5-14, 15-44, and 45-64, which are combined with the reweighted IMR and ECMR. This method therefore only extends the regular Brass-Logit method, which infers values between the two points on a straight line in the log-odds plane, forward and backwards across several points in distinct calculations. This is not without precedent (Moultrie et al. 2013, 350-355). The output values are simply stitched onto each other. The four iterations produce four sets of non-overlapping consecutive individual year lifetable data for each consistent registration district. In some areas the boundaries between each age bracket will provide slight positive or negative discontinuities in the single-year survival probabilities. This is because significant differences in the α and β parameters in different age groups (i.e. a local age bracket’s intensity of deviation from the norm) will produce moderately different q𝑥 values around border years. However, given that imputation uses cumulative lx values it is robust to local shifts in qx. This gives local weighted life tables for ages 0-64 in census years 1851-1911 for 577 consistent districts. The intervening years and quarter years have life tables linearly interpolated between censuses. Each birth cohort of imputable ages now possesses 577 area-specific life tables which reflect the varied mortality landscape of England and Wales. The figure in this file shows the national cohort values for birth cohort survivorship to each age for each census year in red, and each of the 577 districts in black. That the red line sits significantly below the bulk of the districts is expected: it is a population-weighted average dragged down by the mortal conditions in urban areas.
